I recently earned the **AWS Certified AI Practitioner** certification. I've been following and working
with AI and machine learning (ML) on and off for about ten years even though I don't have a formal background
in the field.

One of my earliest memories with ML is downloading a [Word2Vec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word2Vec) model in 
2016 and exploring its capabilities on my own computer. I remember seeing that the difference between the vectors 
for *king* and *queen* was similar to the difference between the vectors for *man* and *woman*. That hooked my 
inner linguistics nerd. I was astonished that a model could learn relationships between words solely based on their 
context. This led me to explore early NLP tasks like sentiment analysis and entity recognition. It 
would be a couple of years before the rise of large language models, but I was already fascinated by the idea of it.

Since then, I've been able to collaborate at work on AI/ML projects in MLOps and application development. I've 
contributed to projects that involved preparing training data and deploying models for inference on AWS Lambda and 
SageMaker. I've worked with bioinformatics teams to package their models in Docker and run them on AWS Batch. More 
recently, I've built agent infrastructure with Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and the Strands Agents SDK, connecting 
agents to internal systems and third-party APIs.

I have had an unconventional path into AI like most people. I've built my understanding over time through curiosity 
and collaborative project work. I took the certification exam to see how well those foundations held up. Passing 
the exam felt like a satisfying milestone in a field I've been curious about for years. If you've taken the exam 
after learning AI through a similarly nontraditional path, feel free to reach out to me by email or on social 
media. If you're thinking about taking it, I explain below what you can expect.

<h2 id="what-is-an-ai-practitioner">What is an AI Practitioner?</h2>

These are questions a team might work through with an AI practitioner when evaluating an AI solution:

**Scenario**

A manager has an idea for an app that identifies common plants from photos people take on a walk.

**Algorithm**

- Could we frame this as image classification and use supervised learning with labeled examples?
- Would a pre-trained model work, or should we train or fine-tune one?
- Would Amazon Rekognition fit this use case?

**Data**

- Are the images unstructured data?
- Will users submit images to the app for real-time inference?
- What data will we use to train or fine-tune the model?
- Could the images or their metadata contain personally identifiable information (PII)?
- What risks of bias might be present in the training data?

**Deployment**

- What services could we use to deploy the model for inference?
- How will we evaluate the model’s performance?
- What privacy and data-protection requirements apply, and how will we meet them?

**Security**

- How will we encrypt training data stored in S3 using KMS?
- How can IAM roles limit each process to only the data it needs?

<h2 id="how-i-prepared">How I prepared</h2>

### Study resources

- **[Udemy course](https://www.udemy.com/course/aws-certified-ai-practitioner/)** by Stéphane Maarek.
- **[Tutorials Dojo practice exams](https://portal.tutorialsdojo.com/courses/aws-certified-ai-practitioner-practice-exams-aif-c01/)**.

I chose these because they were taught by instructors who helped me pass the [Data Engineer Associate exam](/blog/aws-data-engineer-associate).

### My study sequence

1. Took the Udemy section quizzes to see which sections I needed to focus on the most.
2. Watched the Udemy course videos for a week at 1.5x speed and took notes with pencil and paper.
3. Took the Tutorial Dojo section-based practice exams in practice mode, reviewing each question and taking notes on the ones I got wrong.

Fortunately, I was getting passing scores on the Udemy and Tutorial Dojo section quizzes, so I didn't finish all the 
material. I focused on the sections I was less familiar with, like corporate paradigms for **Responsible AI** and 
**Amazon Managed Services for AI**.

<h2 id="what-the-exam-focused-on">What the exam focused on</h2>

You can find the exam guide on the 
[AWS Certified AI Practitioner page](https://aws.amazon.com/certification/certified-ai-practitioner/)
if you want to see the official exam content. These are the topics I remember seeing most often on the exam.

- Responsible AI
  - Including fairness, transparency, and governance. Honestly, I think it's one of the most important parts of
    working with AI, and I was glad the exam spent time on it.
- Machine Learning concepts and terminology
  - I saw questions on supervised vs. unsupervised learning, classification vs. regression, and reinforcement learning.
- AWS managed services for AI/ML
  - Amazon Rekognition, Amazon Comprehend, Amazon Transcribe, Amazon Translate, Amazon Polly, and Amazon Lex. Know what
    each service does and what type of data it works with.
- AI/ML lifecycle
  - I saw questions on data preparation, training, evaluation, and how to deploy a model using features in SageMaker AI.
- Generative AI
  - There were a lot of questions about hallucination, and fine-tuning LLMs using Bedrock and SageMaker JumpStart.

<h2 id="what-surprised-me">What surprised me about the exam</h2>

I was familiar with the scenario-based questions format on the 
[Data Engineer Associate exam](/blog/aws-data-engineer-associate). On this exam, I saw more questions that focused on:

- matching concepts to examples
- remembering ML concepts and terminology
- recommendations for teams with specific needs who are broadly exploring AI
- ordering steps in a process

I had to remind myself that some questions were coming from a manager or project manager perspective. They were asking 
what AI could do at a high level, and not how it is built behind the scenes. Once I reframed the questions in this way, 
it was easier to trust the answer I recognized.

<h2 id="what-i-took-away">What I took away</h2>

Passing the exam confirmed that the foundations I'd built over the years had stuck.
Studying also refreshed the vocabulary I'd use with stakeholders, managers, developers, or data 
scientists. I can talk through what we're trying to solve, what data we'd need, and how we'd know 
whether a model was doing its job without getting deep into the implementation.

There's a whole science behind choosing and training a model. I think of it like working on an engine.
If you're a mechanic, you need to know how the parts fit together and what to check when
something goes wrong. I'm curious how much further the **Machine Learning Engineer - Associate**
exam goes into the algorithmic details and implementation. I guess I'll have to grab that one next. 👀

<h2 id="who-id-recommend-it-to">Who I'd recommend it to</h2>

- **If you're new to AI**, I'd recommend it if you want to feel more comfortable with the terms
  that come up in conversations about models and how they're used in AWS.
- **If you've learned AI through project work**, it's a chance to check how well you can explain
  the concepts behind what you've been doing and refresh the ones you use less often.
