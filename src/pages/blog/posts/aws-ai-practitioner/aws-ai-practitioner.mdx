I recently earned the **AWS Certified AI Practitioner** certification. I've been following and working
with AI and machine learning (ML) on and off for about ten years even though I don't have a formal background
in the field.

One of my earliest memories with ML is downloading a [Word2Vec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word2Vec) model in 2016, trying it on my own computer. I remember 
seeing that the difference between the vectors for *king* and *queen* was similar to the difference between 
the vectors for *man* and *woman*. That hooked my inner linguistics nerd. I was astonished that a model could 
learn relationships between words from the contexts in which they appear across a large collection of text. 
It would be a couple years before the rise of large language models, but I was already fascinated by the 
idea of it.

My professional AI work has focused primarily on MLOps and application development. I've prepared training data 
and deployed Dockerized models for inference on AWS Lambda and SageMaker. I've also partnered with bioinformatics 
teams to package their models in Docker and run them on AWS Batch. More recently, I've built agent infrastructure 
with Amazon Bedrock AgentCore and the Strands Agents SDK, connecting agents to internal systems and third-party APIs.

I have had an unconventional path into AI. I've built my understanding over time through curiosity and collaborative 
project work. I took the certification exam to see how well those foundations held up. Earning the credential felt 
like a satisfying milestone in a field I've been curious about for years. If you've taken the exam after learning 
AI through a similarly nontraditional path, feel free to reach out to me by email or on social media. If you're 
thinking about taking it, I explain below what you can expect.

<h2 id="how-i-prepared">How I prepared</h2>

### Study resources

- **[Udemy course](https://www.udemy.com/course/aws-certified-ai-practitioner/)** by Stéphane Maarek.
- **[Tutorials Dojo practice exams](https://portal.tutorialsdojo.com/courses/aws-certified-ai-practitioner-practice-exams-aif-c01/)**.

I chose these because they were taught by instructors who helped me pass the [Data Engineer Associate exam](/blog/aws-data-engineer-associate).

### My study sequence

1. Took the Udemy section quizzes to see which sections I needed to focus on the most.
2. Watched the Udemy course videos for a week at 1.5x speed. Taking notes with pencil and paper.
3. Took the Tutorial Dojo section-based practice exams in practice mode, reviewing each question and taking notes on the ones I got wrong.

Fortunately, I was getting passing scores on the Udemy and Tutorial Dojo section quizzes, so I didn't finish all the 
material. I focused on the sections I was less familiar with, like corporate paradigms for **Responsible AI** and 
**Amazon Managed Services for AI**.

<h2 id="what-surprised-me">What surprised me about the exam</h2>

For the [Data Engineer Associate exam](/blog/aws-data-engineer-associate), I became familiar with the scenario based 
formula question format. It involved reading a scenario, picking out the constraints, and working through the tradeoffs. 

On this exam, I saw more matching questions and straightforward questions that asked me to recall a definition. I'd 
read a simple question and start looking for a catch, then talk myself out of the answer I'd recognized right away. 
I had to remind myself that some questions were coming from a manager or sales perspective. They were asking what AI 
could do at a high level, not how I'd build it behind the scenes. Once I reframed the question that way, it was 
easier to trust the answer I recognized.

<h2 id="what-the-exam-focused-on">What the exam focused on</h2>

### Machine learning and AWS services

I had to recognize the basic machine learning approaches and the kinds of data they use. There were also questions about where training, evaluation, deployment, and monitoring fit in the model lifecycle. For AWS services, the key was matching the service to the task. For example, **Amazon Transcribe** turns a recording into text.

### Responsible AI

Fairness, transparency, and governance came up often too. The questions made me think about what a team needs to decide before putting a model to use:

- How will we check whether the model performs worse for some groups?
- What will we tell people about how the model is being used?
- Who's responsible for following up if we find bias?

Those answers can affect how the team collects data, evaluates the model, and decides where to use it, so it's better to think them through early.

<h2 id="what-i-took-away">What I took away</h2>

Passing the exam confirmed that the foundations I'd built over the years had stuck.
Studying also refreshed the vocabulary I'd use in a meeting with business stakeholders,
developers, or data scientists. I can talk through what we're trying to solve, what data we'd need,
and how we'd know whether a model was doing its job without getting deep into the implementation.

There's a whole science behind choosing and training a model. I think of it like working on an engine.
Once you're the mechanic, you need to know how the parts fit together and what to check when
something goes wrong. I'm curious how much further the **Machine Learning Engineer – Associate**
exam goes into that work. I guess I'll have to grab that one next. :)

<h2 id="who-id-recommend-it-to">Who I'd recommend it to</h2>

- **If you're new to AI**, I'd recommend it if you want to feel more comfortable with the terms
  that come up in conversations about models and how they're used.
- **If you've learned AI through project work**, it's a chance to check how well you can explain
  the concepts behind what you've been doing and refresh the ones you use less often.
